# Week 2 notes: duty and proximate cause

A negligence claim has four elements: duty, breach, causation (cause in fact and
proximate cause) and damages. The plaintiff must prove each one.

## Duty and the foreseeable plaintiff

In *Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928),
railroad guards helped a passenger board a moving train; he dropped a package of
fireworks, which exploded, and the shock knocked over scales that injured Mrs.
Palsgraf at the far end of the platform. Chief Judge Cardozo held there was no
liability: the duty of care is owed only to those within the zone of reasonably
foreseeable danger. Negligence "in the air" is not enough. Judge Andrews' dissent
framed the limit as one of proximate cause instead, asking whether the result was
too remote as a matter of policy and common sense.

## Duty without privity

In *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.*, 217 N.Y. 382, 111 N.E. 1050 (1916), a wheel
on a car collapsed and injured the buyer, who had bought from a dealer, not from
Buick. Cardozo held that a manufacturer owes a duty of care to foreseeable users
of a product that is reasonably certain to place life and limb in peril when
negligently made, whether or not they are in privity of contract.

## Working method

1. Identify who was foreseeably endangered by the defendant's conduct.
2. Ask whether this plaintiff falls within that zone (Cardozo) or whether the
   harm is too remote (Andrews).
3. Address the strongest counterargument before predicting.
